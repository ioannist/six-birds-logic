\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Most accounts of physical computation start from bits, registers, and truth tables, and then ask how a substrate implements them and at what thermodynamic cost \cite{landauer1961,bennett1973,fredkintoffoli1982conservative,bennett1982thermodynamics}. This paper asks an earlier question: when does a coarse-grained description of a system deserve to be called logical at all? Any system can be given labels that look like \(0\) and \(1\). The labels may still fail to persist, the dynamics may not act on them as a well-defined rule, and the description may silently discard information that the system still carries. In such cases logic has been projected onto the system, not found in it.

Our answer is operational. We work in the Six Birds emergence calculus \cite{tsiokos2026sixbirds}, which describes how new layers of description arise from six primitives: operator rewrite (P1), gating and constraints (P2), protocol holonomy (P3), sectors and invariants (P4), packaging (P5), and accounting and audit (P6). Logic is not a seventh primitive in this picture. It is something these six can jointly produce. A system has a \emph{logic layer} when packaged binary predicates persist, when the dynamics induces well-defined update rules on them, and when the reliability and the losses of that closure are measured. In one sentence: a logic layer is an audited closure of packaged predicates under induced dynamics.

The definition has two sides. On the semantic side, a proposition is a set of microstates that the layer can express, and the connectives are set operations on those sets. On the operational side, a bit is an equivalence class of microstates, and a gate is an update that the micro-dynamics induces on such classes. The first side says what the layer's statements mean; the second says whether the substrate can execute them. Replacing ``how is logic implemented?'' with ``when has logic emerged strongly enough to close?'' connects the question to classical work on rough sets and partition logic \cite{pawlak1982rough,ellerman2010partitions} and to the theory of macrostates and coarse variables \cite{shalizi2025macrostate}.

The title emphasizes XOR for a concrete reason. Variables aligned with a system's sector or invariant structure are often especially good coarse variables \cite{simonando1961aggregation,shalizi2025macrostate,barnettseth2023dynamical}, and parity is the simplest such variable. Parity is not metaphysically special. It is a clean test case for the paper's broader claim that some logical distinctions are easier for a substrate to stabilize than others. The useful question is therefore not whether a substrate can be made to emulate a given truth table in principle, but which distinctions it supports natively, how robustly, and at what accounting cost.

To make the definition testable we introduce a small suite of diagnostics: predicate stability, route mismatch, closure defect, gate error, conditional entropy, unretained input information, and entropy production. Together they ask whether a packaged bit persists long enough to matter, whether microstates that share a label also share a coarse future, whether a gate is a closed operator or an artifact of how it was evaluated, and whether information that seems to disappear has been destroyed or merely left out of the description. Formally, the descent of dynamics to coarse variables is the lumpability problem for Markov chains \cite{kemeny1976finite,buchholz1994lumpability,simonando1961aggregation,givon2004extracting}; the comparison of retained and erased descriptions connects to the literature on irreversibility and reversible computation \cite{landauer1961,bennett1973,bennett1982thermodynamics}. Within the Six Birds program, the paper builds on the foundations in \cite{tsiokos2026sixbirds} and on related closure analyses in \cite{tsiokos2026countstone,tsiokos2026minsubstrate}.

We apply the diagnostics to finite Markov laboratories in which NOT, AND, and reversible XOR (CNOT) gates run with tunable noise, metastability, and coarse-graining, and ask four questions. Which binary coarse variables close most robustly? How do familiar gates behave as noise, staging barriers, and evaluation horizons vary? What changes when a reversible gate is read through a simpler but lossy output? And can stable bits and gates be recovered from transition structure without being declared in advance?

The answers are as follows. In a laboratory built around leaky parity sectors, the parity bit has zero route mismatch at every leakage rate and horizon, whereas every other balanced binary partition and the AND predicate have strictly positive mismatch at every tested leakage rate and horizon, with closed-form expressions for each (Section~\ref{sec:results-parity}). NOT and AND lose closure when their stored inputs drift over long horizons, whereas CNOT separates two properties that usually travel together: a noiseless one-step CNOT is an exact input--output channel, yet its output bit, read alone, has maximal route mismatch (Section~\ref{sec:results-phase}). Reading one CNOT laboratory through its XOR output rather than its full register multiplies the unretained input information by \(8.07\) and raises route mismatch from \(0\) to \(0.96\), so the reversible gate and its erased view are different logical objects rather than one object described at two resolutions (Section~\ref{sec:results-reversible}). Finally, a spectral and information-theoretic pipeline recovers the intended stable bits and a NOT gate directly from the transition kernel (Section~\ref{sec:results-discovery}).

These results have different epistemic status, and we keep them apart. The predicate and quotient facts behind the framework, such as the closure of definable predicates under the Boolean connectives and the descent of updates that respect a partition, are proved in Lean~4. The main laboratory quantities, including the parity comparison, the reversible-versus-erased comparison, and the gate channels in every condition of the phase diagram, are re-derived independently in closed form. Their probabilities and route mismatches are checked in exact rational arithmetic, using the recorded decimal values of the barrier-dependent memory noise in the phase grid; Shannon quantities are checked numerically, and the eightfold loss inequality is certified with rational logarithm bounds (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-lean}). Everything else comes from floating-point computation and is reported as such.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
The paper makes three contributions. First, it gives a layer-relative definition of logic within the Six Birds framework: a logic layer is an audited closure of packaged predicates under induced dynamics. Second, it provides diagnostics that test each clause of that definition and separate closed logic layers from symbolic descriptions that merely fit. Third, it demonstrates the framework in controlled finite laboratories, establishing exact parity closure, a separation between reversible and erased views of one gate, and unsupervised recovery of packaged bits and a NOT gate.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The paper is neither a survey of physical computation nor a claim that Boolean logic is the only possible outcome of coarse-graining. In the set-based laboratories studied here, the events a lens can express always form a Boolean algebra, so complement is never unavailable as a statement. What a layer can lack is the ability to keep its bits stable and to execute operations on them, and these are what the diagnostics measure. Showing that a layer has a genuinely non-Boolean event structure would require restricted operations or richer semantics, which we do not develop here. The laboratories are designed for diagnostic clarity rather than physical realism, and Definition~\ref{def:logic-layer} is an operational criterion whose tolerances are left to the application.
