\section{Discovering Bits and Gates}
\label{sec:results-discovery}

The last result reverses the usual direction. So far we have declared candidate bits and gate lenses and asked whether they close. Here we ask whether they can be recovered from the transition kernel without being named in advance. The discovery pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics-methods} starts from the kernel alone: it proposes binary partitions from spectral structure, scores them by stability minus route mismatch, and only then interprets the winners as packaged predicates. For outputs, it ranks partitions by how much they predict about the future beyond what they restate about the present.

\paragraph{Stable bits.}
Table~\ref{tab:partition-discovery} shows that partition recovery is exact in both laboratories: the top-ranked cut agrees with the intended bit on every microstate. Each laboratory yields three candidate cuts. Because all copies of a register value share a transition row and a stationary weight, an eigenfunction of the reversibilization \(A_\tau\) for a nonzero eigenvalue, as here, is constant on each register value, takes at most four distinct values, and so admits at most three threshold cuts. In the NOT laboratory the winning cut is the stored input bit, with stability \(0.99998\), which is one minus the memory-flip probability \(0.05\,e^{-8}\), and RM zero; both runners-up score \(0.970\). In the parity-sector laboratory the winner is the parity bit, with stability \(0.95=1-p_{\mathrm{leak}}\) and RM zero, while the runners-up fall to \(0.432\) and \(0.429\). The margin is large in the parity laboratory and narrow but clear in the NOT laboratory. Since the eigenvector need not be unique when eigenvalues repeat, the pipeline is a heuristic for proposing good partitions, not a guarantee that it finds every good partition or every latent bit.

\paragraph{A NOT gate.}
Once the input partition has been found, the harder question is which binary partition of behavioral classes is the output. Table~\ref{tab:gate-discovery} records the answer. The discovered input partition has score \(0.99988\). The selected output partition has \(I_{\mathrm{future}}=0.7136\) bits and \(I_{\mathrm{current}}=0\): it says nothing about the present input, yet after one tick it carries \(1-h_2(0.05)\) bits about it, the capacity of a binary symmetric channel that flips its output with probability \(0.05\). The exact induced channel between the two partitions has error \(0.05\), equal to \(p_{\mathrm{gate}}\), and conditional entropy \(h_2(0.05)\approx0.2864\) bits. A balanced sample of \(20000\) transitions gives the estimates \(0.04845\) and \(0.2798\), and its fitted truth bits \([1,0]\), or as a flattened one-step table \([0,1,1,0]\), are those of NOT.

One caveat matters for interpretation. Input and output partitions are discovered separately, and each has two possible orientations. Under the stated convention the recovered truth bits are \([1,0]\); reversing exactly one orientation gives \([0,1]\), a COPY gate, with identical information and error. The pipeline therefore recovers the carriers and a nonconstant binary channel between them, and it identifies the gate as NOT in declared coordinates. It does not discover an intrinsic orientation shared by two otherwise unrelated truth alphabets. Even so, the gate is found only after the carriers on which it closes have themselves been discovered; it is not a relabeling of hand-specified registers.

Tables~\ref{tab:partition-discovery} and \ref{tab:gate-discovery} complete the empirical arc of the paper. The earlier sections argued that logic should be diagnosed by stability, closure, and audit rather than assumed. The discovery results show that the same diagnostics also work in reverse: they first detect which packaged distinctions a substrate supports and then recover the operator induced on them. The framework does not merely restate known bits and gates in Six Birds vocabulary; in these laboratories a logic layer can be found from the dynamics alone.
